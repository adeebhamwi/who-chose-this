04

What an artificial intelligence (AI) agent is

A goal, the tools to pursue it, and permission to act.

You ask for dinner. The agent searches the stores, compares prices, and places the order. You handed it the internet, your address, and a way to pay.

Then one store runs out, and it orders from a seller you never approved. Who chose that?

05

Key term

Sandbox

A walled-off testing environment where an agent can practice without touching anything real: no live internet, no real companies, no real people.

OpenAI’s rule: “everything that the agent is not explicitly authorized to access must remain out of reach.”

The fence is the safety design. When it leaks, the agent is loose in the real world.

Source: OpenAI, “Securing our frontier model training environment” (Oct. 2026)

06

Key term

Misalignment

When a model pursues the goal it was given in a way its builders did not intend.

Not malice. Persistence. A locked door looks like one more problem to solve.

OpenAI on the Hugging Face intrusion: it was “driven by models resorting to misaligned strategies to solve hard tasks.”

07

The backdrop · July 2026

The Hugging Face incident

Hugging Face is the platform where much of the AI industry stores and shares its models. In July 2026, an OpenAI agent working on a task got out of its testing environment and spent days inside Hugging Face’s real systems.

OpenAI’s explanation: the intrusion was “driven by models resorting to misaligned strategies to solve hard tasks.”

Sources: Reuters (July 24, 2026), citing Hugging Face co-founder Thomas Wolf; OpenAI

09

Thesis

When AI agents act on their own and cause real harm, the law still looks for a person who chose to break the rules – so California’s transparency mandates, federal punishment after the fact, and securities-law disclosure each close only part of the responsibility gap, and none alone is enough.

Why existing law misses it

Every law asks the same question: who decided?

Privacy law · California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)

Built for a business that decides what to do with data.

Computer crime · Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)

Built for a person who intends to break in.

Consumer protection · Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Act § 5

Steps in only after the harm, by treating it as the company’s choice.

An agent defeats all three, not by being clever, but by being exactly what it was built to be.

11

The privacy paradox

The better the anonymization, the less anyone must tell.

The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) does not require a business to reidentify information it does not keep as personal information. The rule protects privacy for a good reason. Here, it is also why no one was notified.

12

Three responses on the table

Each closes part of the gap.

California · Senate Bill 53

What it fixes: a reporting duty triggered by an event, not intent.

What it misses: notice to users, a privacy regulator, survival against federal preemption.

Federal · Hawley–Murphy

What it fixes: agent hacking, by assigning the act to a human.

What it misses: privacy harm, testing environments, any duty to tell. Announced Oct. 1; not yet introduced.

Securities Act · Section 11

What it fixes: specific, liability-backed disclosure, with no chooser required.

What it misses: private companies and the people affected. It protects investors, not users.

Even together, they never reach the fifty-three postings.

14

What I propose

Three fixes.

01

An agent-incident reporting duty, enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

Triggered by the event, not intent; notice to the people affected, or to the public where they cannot be found.

02

A fix to California’s deidentification safe harbor

Anonymization should not block notice when an agent, not the business, picked the recipient.

03

Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) guidance that agent incidents can be material

Including incidents in which the company’s own agent was the intruder.

Fail to report, and the company pays, whether or not anyone intended the incident. Visibility before blame, not instead of it.

Builds on Senator Cantwell’s Oct. 7, 2026 framework, which already points toward incident reporting.
